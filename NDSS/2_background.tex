\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section reviews the domain background and the prior work that motivate our design. We describe the PKI standards stack and its multi-tier constraints on certificate format, summarize the static compliance-checking tools whose limitations motivate this study, and survey related work on specification extraction, knowledge-graph retrieval, and LLM output control.

\subsection{X.509 Certificate Standard}
\label{sec:bg-pki}

X.509 certificates are the primary data representation in the Web PKI. Their structure and validation semantics are defined across layered specifications and policy frameworks. IETF~RFC~5280 defines the foundational layer, including the certificate ASN.1 structure, field-level semantics, and the path-validation algorithm. Above it, the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements, or BR, impose operational and configuration constraints on commercial CAs that issue publicly trusted TLS certificates. The ETSI~EN\,319 series adds EU-specific configuration profiles aligned with the eIDAS trust framework, and the Mozilla Root Store Policy, or MRSP, occupies the browser-policy layer with its own technical requirements. Together, these four normative sources form the PKI constraint stack. The layers cross-reference one another and sometimes impose local overrides. Chapter~7 of CABF~BR, for example, references RFC~5280 directly while tightening individual field-level constraints.

A certificate is a signed ASN.1 object whose core payload is the tbsCertificate structure. This payload contains identity and validity metadata, including version, serialNumber, signature, issuer, validity, subject, and subjectPublicKeyInfo. Version~3 certificates further carry an extension sequence, which is where many security-relevant Web PKI constraints reside. Common examples include subjectAltName for DNS names and email names, basicConstraints for CA status and path-length limits, keyUsage and extendedKeyUsage for allowed cryptographic purposes, certificatePolicies for policy identifiers, and authority-information or revocation pointers such as AIA and CRL distribution points. These fields are encoded as DER bytes, but normative rules are usually written as natural-language statements over semantic field names, profiles, and certificate roles.

\subsection{Static Certificate Compliance Checking}
\label{sec:bg-linting}

Web PKI certificate issuance compliance is checked primarily with static certificate linting tools. These linting systems inspect the encoded contents of individual certificates and do not require external validation state. zlint~\cite{kumar2018tracking} is the most widely deployed static linter. pkilint~\cite{pkilint}, certlint~\cite{certlint}, and x509lint~\cite{x509lint} extend this ecosystem further.

zlint organizes checks as individually registered lints with metadata such as citation, effective date, and severity; each lint implements a predicate over parsed certificate structures and is commonly exercised through pass/error test certificates.

This design has two important implications for our work. First, a lint check is not a direct copy of a normative source sentence. It is an engineering artifact that embeds a chosen field path, a predicate, a severity level, and often a scope condition. Second, the absence of a lint check does not necessarily mean that no normative rule exists; it may mean that the normative rule has not been recognized as lintable or has not yet been implemented.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:bg-related}

Automated rule extraction has been studied in privacy, legal, and protocol specifications, but these settings differ from Web PKI issuance compliance. PolicyLint~\cite{andow2019policylint} and PrivacyFlash~Pro~\cite{zimmeck2021privacyflash} analyze privacy text, and Hassani et al.~\cite{hassani2024rethinking} leverage large language models for legal-compliance analysis. These methods identify obligations or data-practice constraints, but they do not map normative rules to DER-encoded certificate fields or decide whether a rule is enforceable by inspecting one issued certificate. N-Check~\cite{feng2024analyzing} formalizes normative rules as FOL$^*$ formulas, but its target is well-formedness analysis rather than lint predicates over X.509 field paths.

LLM-based standard-analysis systems show that technical specifications can be parsed automatically, while leaving a different gap. PROSPER~\cite{sharma2023prosper}, ParCleanse~\cite{zheng2025validating}, and SpecGPT~\cite{zhang2025automated} extract RFC state machines, protocol formats, and 3GPP state-machine behavior. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025uncovering} use GraphRAG-style context~\cite{edge2024local,peng2025graph} to align RFC text with kernel code. These works focus on states, formats, or implementation differentials; they do not separate exhaustive normative-rule recall, schema-constrained IR parsing, and deterministic lintability decision for PKI issuance normative rules.
RFCAUDIT~\cite{zheng2025rfcaudit} is especially close in using hierarchical code-semantic indexing and retrieval-guided LLM analysis to align RFC requirements with protocol implementations and identify functional inconsistencies. However, it analyzes whether existing code satisfies extracted protocol properties, whereas our pipeline first seeks exhaustive normative-rule recall, determines single-certificate lintability, and generates restricted lint predicates for the lintable subset.

Recent systems connect natural-language protocol specifications to code more
directly. SPEC2CODE~\cite{wang2025spec2code} maps fine-grained RFC requirements
to function-level implementations for consistency analysis, while
ProtocolGuard~\cite{song2026protocolguard} extracts normative rules and combines
LLM-guided static analysis with dynamic verification to detect non-compliance in
existing protocol implementations. In the generative direction, LLMs
Unleashed~\cite{long2026llms} generates complete protocol implementations from
RFCs. These systems either analyze an existing implementation or generate
protocol code; our setting instead determines whether an issuance requirement
is observable from one certificate and generates a restricted zlint predicate
for that requirement.

Output-control and formal-verification work is also complementary. XGrammar~\cite{dong2025xgrammar} and CRANE~\cite{banerjee2025crane} constrain LLM outputs through grammars, while Schall and de~Melo~\cite{schall2025hidden} show that constrained decoding can hurt reasoning quality. ARMOR~\cite{debnath2024armor} verifies the RFC~5280 path-validation algorithm in Agda. These approaches improve output form or algorithm assurance, but they do not determine which natural-language issuance rules should become static lint checks.

In the certificate and message-format domain, code generation has instead been specification-driven: EverParse~\cite{ramananandro2019everparse} and Nail~\cite{bangert2014nail} emit verified parsers and serializers from a declarative format grammar. These systems produce correct-by-construction format handlers, but their input is a hand-written formal grammar rather than natural-language normative text, and their output is a parser rather than a lint predicate that decides issuance compliance. Our setting instead consumes schema-constrained IR distilled from normative sentences and emits executable lint checks whose synonymy with the source rule is separately checked.

Certificate issuance compliance measurement is closest to our empirical setting. Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2018tracking} measured certificate misissuance at ecosystem scale, and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025unicert} identified Unicode-related issuance and parsing non-compliance in internationalized X.509 certificates; these studies show that issuance failures are measurable and operationally important, but they start from known lint checks or defect classes. On the validation side, Frankencerts~\cite{brubaker2014frankencerts} synthesizes adversarial certificates for differential testing of SSL/TLS certificate validation, and SymCerts~\cite{chau2017symcerts} uses symbolic execution to expose noncompliance in X.509 validation implementations; both exercise validator code paths rather than deciding, from normative text, which issuance rules can be captured by a static lint.
