\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section runs experiments with the methods and framework above and draws
quantitative conclusions. The internal layered metrics are the lint-coverage
analysis of \S\ref{subsec:coverage} and the code-generation results of
\S\ref{subsec:codegen-results}. Beyond them, two mutually independent external
validations each check one half of the framework. \S\ref{subsec:lintability-ext}
externally validates normative-rule extraction and lintability determination against the
zlint maintainers' manual gold standard, and \S\ref{subsec:certdetect} externally
validates code generation and semantic-equivalence judgment by execution on real
certificates.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

All experiments use GPT-5.4 as the only LLM. The LLM judges are configured at temperature~$0$. We target
RFC~5280 and CABF~BR because they are
the two most authoritative and foundational Web PKI normative sources for publicly
trusted TLS certificates. RFC~5280 defines the base X.509 certificate profile that
virtually every downstream normative rule inherits, and CABF~BR is the baseline that every
publicly trusted CA is mandated to meet; it also builds directly on RFC~5280, so
together they bound where compliance linting matters most. Normative-rule extraction was run on CABF~BR~v2.2.6. This was the latest
CABF~BR release when the experiments began.

\subsection{Lint Coverage Analysis}
\label{subsec:coverage}

This subsection measures how many of our lintable normative rules an existing tool already
implements. We restrict the analysis to zlint, the most widely deployed
certificate linter, integrated into major CA issuance and CT-monitoring
pipelines. The criterion is deliberately strict. A lintable normative rule counts as
covered only when some cognate, or provenance-matched, zlint lint genuinely implements it, not merely
mentions the same field. We accordingly label each normative rule full, partial, or none by
whether such a lint implements it fully, partially, or not at all, and count only
full as covered.

Our matching criterion requires information beyond a metadata description
alone. We therefore first summarize each lint into a reverse IR in Stage-0. For
every lint we pass three inputs to an offline summarizer
$M_{\mathrm{summ}}$ under a constrained prompt: the lint's Go source code,
its Pass/Error test certificates, and the description, citation, and severity
metadata fields. From these we recover the same IR schema our normative rules use, the core
four-tuple $\langle$subject, obligation, predicate, constraint$\rangle$ plus a
one-sentence code summary. Three considerations motivate these inputs. First, the
CheckApplies and Execute branches expose the concrete field and threshold the lint
actually tests. Second, the
Pass/Error samples fix the direction of the check, that is, which values it
accepts and which it rejects, together with its applicable scope. Third, casting
the lint into our four-tuple lets each normative rule and lint be compared field by field.
Extracting the full four-tuple, not just the code summary sentence, is what lets
Stage-2 compare subject, obligation, predicate, and constraint jointly and reach a
defensible full-versus-partial distinction.

\input{algorithms/alg_coverage}

Given these reverse IRs, Algorithm~\ref{alg:coverage} aligns each normative rule to zlint in
four further stages that combine deterministic steps with a single controlled
judge. Stage-1 retrieves candidate lints for a normative rule by provenance. A
normative rule from RFC~5280 is matched only against RFC lints whose cited section shares a common prefix
with the normative rule's section, a test we write \textsc{SectionPrefixMatch}, since RFC
section numbers are stable. Because CABF section numbers drift across versions,
every CABF lint is taken as a candidate for a CABF normative rule. Stage-2 hands the normative rule's four-tuple and its candidate set to a
field-level judge $M_{\mathrm{judge}}$, which compares the tuples field by
field and returns the best candidate with a full, partial, or none verdict.
Stage-3 is a deterministic false-positive guard. It maps the subject of the normative rule
and of the selected lint to a field family such as KeyUsage or SubjectAltName via
$\mathrm{Family}(\cdot)$, and downgrades a full or partial verdict to none whenever
the two resolve to different nonempty families, so a KeyUsage normative rule is never
credited to a lint that checks SubjectAltName merely because both mention an
extension. Stage-4 aggregates deterministically. A normative rule is covered if and only if its verdict
is full, and the uncovered remainder forms the code-generation domain $\RLunc$. The LLM is invoked
only at Stage-0 and Stage-2; candidate retrieval, the family guard, and
aggregation are fully deterministic.

Of the 260 lintable normative rules, zlint fully covers 165 and leaves 95 uncovered under
this procedure, as Table~\ref{tab:coverage} reports. The 95 uncovered normative rules are
the code-generation domain $\RLunc$. The 292 cognate lints and the 165 covered
normative rules count different objects and should not be read as a ratio. The 292 is the
count of cognate zlint lints, whereas 165 is the number of our lintable normative rules that
some cognate lint fully implements. Two effects hold the covered normative-rule count well
below the cognate-lint count. First, several zlint lints often target the same
certificate field or obligation, so multiple cognate lints map to a single normative rule,
most visibly on RFC~5280 where 122 cognate lints correspond to only 85 lintable
normative rules. Second, the criterion credits a normative rule only when a cognate lint implements it
fully, so lints that merely partially match, or that the deterministic family
guard downgrades to none, stay in the pool without adding any covered normative rule.

The first-layer quality signal is G1 conservation, stated as
Theorem~\ref{thm:recall} in \S\ref{subsec:targets}. For the denominator accounting above,
conservation holds strictly, instantiating the two appendix
identities~\eqref{eq:cons} and~\eqref{eq:split}:
\[
\begin{array}{c @{\;} c @{\;} c @{\;} c @{\;} c @{\;} c @{\;} c}
  \underset{|\Rkw|}{2080} & = & \underset{|\RN|\ \text{noise}}{513} & + & \underset{|\RL|\ \text{lintable}}{260} & + & \underset{|\RU|\ \text{not-lintable}}{1307} \\[9pt]
  \underset{|\RL|}{260} & = & \underset{|\RLcov|\ \text{covered}}{165} & + & \underset{|\RLunc|\ \text{uncovered}}{95}
\end{array}
\]
Both identities hold term by term, so the G1 residual $\Lrec = 0$ and the top two
conservation layers are closed and deterministically recomputable. The first layer
is the three-way recall split of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cons}, and the second is the
covered/uncovered split of the lintable set in Eq.~\eqref{eq:split}. The 260
lintable normative rules further split by normative source into 175 CABF and 85 RFC~5280, and
the 95 uncovered normative rules $\RLunc$ are fed to the downstream code generator.

\subsection{External Validation of Normative-Rule Extraction and Lintability
Determination}
\label{subsec:lintability-ext}

Normative-rule extraction and lintability determination need an independent external
reference. For this we bring in the CABF BR mapping table published by zlint
maintainers~\cite{zlint-mapping-sheet}. This table is manually annotated for each BR
normative rule by zlint experts as to whether it can be expressed by a certificate lint,
and the annotators are unaware of our extractor, IR, and lintability determination
module. Each Yes/No label in the table corresponds exactly to our joint output
of extracting that normative rule and determining whether it is lintable, and
agreement indicates that our normative-rule extraction and lintability determination match
human experts. The table covers two versions, BR~1.4.8~\cite{cabf-br148-md} and
BR~2.0.2~\cite{cabf-br202-md}, compared version-for-version against our
CABF-Server-1.4.8 extraction of 422 normative rules and the CABF-Server-2.0.2 extraction of 1024
normative rules, both produced by the same two-layer pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:extval}
reports the version-for-version agreement.

A small number of sheet rows fall outside our scope, so match coverage is near
87\% on both versions. These rows are title-case or lowercase descriptors such as
``Required if'', ``Deprecated'', or lowercase ``shall'' that RFC~2119 processing
correctly skips, together with, on the BR~2.0.2 sheet, cross-references or chapter
anchors with no matching extracted clause. On BR~2.0.2 the matched positive class is
very small, just 12 of 217 matched pairs (5.5\%), so a naive all-No baseline
already attains 94.5\% agreement. The more meaningful figure is therefore
$\kappa = 0.616$, substantial agreement on the Landis--Koch
scale~\cite{landis1977measurement}. BR~1.4.8 has more balanced classes, with 69
of 106 matched pairs positive (65\%), and yields $\kappa = 0.823$,
almost-perfect agreement and a tighter estimate of system quality.

\subsection{Certificate Detection}
\label{subsec:certdetect}

Injecting the 79 final accepted lints into the zlint binary and scanning
certificates checks code generation and semantic-equivalence judgment. We count a hit as a
valid finding only when three conditions hold. First, the final zlint code's
semantics have been checked back to the source text. Second, the certificate
yields a non-pass result. Third, an independent structural audit re-derives the
same defect from the DER or openssl. On external corpora we additionally require
that no native zlint lint flags the same certificate, so that existing coverage is
not miscredited to a generated lint. In Table~\ref{tab:certfind}, rows are
the five finding types, and numeric cells report confirmed certificate-problem
instances for the corresponding corpus.

The five rows of Table~\ref{tab:certfind} are the five confirmed finding types
observed across all evaluated corpora. The testdata column sums to 101
independently confirmed fixture problem instances. The underlying audit file
contains 137 confirmed row-level fixture violations because some defects are
represented by two source rows. We read severity conservatively.
The external AKI finding and the fixture-only anyPolicy findings are Error-level
profile faults whose direct cryptographic consequence is indirect, while the Root
CA CRLDP finding is a lower, Warn-level advisory.

The corpus first uses zlint's own testdata as a gate rather than an ecosystem
estimate. On 1128 parsable certificates the 79 lints raise 2962 raw
hits. Of these, 137 row-level fixture violations are independently confirmed,
while 2825 are NOCHECK, meaning no independent check applies to that lint family, so
they are neither confirmed nor refuted and are not claimed. To test whether these findings exist only
in hand-constructed testdata, we scan two external corpora. The first is the
TLS-handshake certificate chains of the Tranco Top~1M domains, 47,791 deduplicated
PEMs, and the second is a recent sample of CT logs, 63,327 deduplicated PEMs. The
raw generated-lint hit counts are large, 57,583 on Tranco and 80,021 on CT.

The three confirmation conditions collapse these raw counts sharply. Discarding
every hit on a certificate that some native zlint lint already flags removes the
bulk of them, because most raw matches land on certificates that existing coverage
also rejects, and leaves 1042 candidates on Tranco and 167 on CT. The independent
structural audit then re-derives each surviving defect from the DER and confirms 4
certificate-problem instances on Tranco and 1 on CT, marking the remainder NOCHECK
and therefore not claiming those rows. The externally confirmed findings summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:certfind} each escape native zlint coverage and reproduce under an
independent audit, so the reading stays conservative and no finding rests on the
generated lint alone.

Overall, the final accepted lints confirm five finding types across the evaluated
corpora, including findings in deployed certificates that native zlint misses.
All five trace to GENERIC-participating lints.
Every claimed finding survives both a source-text re-check and an independent
structural audit.
This indicates that our code generation and semantic-equivalence judgment produce checks
that pass the code--normative-rule semantic-equivalence gate and are useful on deployed
certificates.
